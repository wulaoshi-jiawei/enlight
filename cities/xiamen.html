Xiamen

Xiamen is a southern door on the Fujian coast, with the sea close at hand and a softness in the air that the north does not have. Just across the water lies Gulangyu — an island of red-tiled villas, shady lanes, gardens and pianos, set apart from the newer city and still best reached on foot. The old concessions left their mark here, but so did the sea trade that has always tied Fujian to Southeast Asia.

Come in through Xiamen and you are already in the south: the coast, the oyster farms, the temple courtyards open to the breeze. From here a short flight or the rail line takes you north toward the heartland, or further into the south.

When to go & weather

Xiamen is mild in winter and warm for much of the year. Autumn and late spring are the most comfortable months; midsummer is hot, humid, and the season when typhoons are most likely.

Travel Guides

How to get there

By air

Xiamen Gaoqi International Airport (XMN) is close to the city, with strong connections across China and a useful network into Southeast Asia. The airport is near enough that the transfer into town is short.

By train

Xiamen and Xiamen North stations sit on the coastal high-speed line, with services north toward Fuzhou, Hangzhou and Shanghai, and inland toward the rest of the network.

Getting around

The metro, buses, taxis and Didi cover the island city itself. Gulangyu is reached by ferry; once you are there, the island is car-free, and the only way to see it is on foot. A half day is too little; give it a night if you can.

Best time to visit

October to December, and again in March and April, are the most pleasant months: warm enough for the waterfront, without the heavy heat of summer. From May the humidity rises. July to September can bring typhoons; if you travel then, leave a little flexibility in the plan.

Places Of Interest

Gulangyu

A short ferry from the city, Gulangyu is a world apart: colonial villas under old trees, winding lanes, a famous piano tradition, and views back across the strait to the newer skyline. There are no cars. The island rewards a slow day, and a night after the day-trippers have gone.

The waterfront and the older streets

Along the coast of the main island, the promenade and the remaining older streets still have the air of a port city: fishing boats, seafood stalls, and a habit of living outdoors in the evening breeze.

Nanputuo Temple

At the foot of a low hill, near the university, Nanputuo is one of the important Buddhist temples of the south: courtyards open to the air, incense in the shade, and a climb behind the halls for a view over the city and the sea.

Xiamen is a southern beginning. From here the way still leads inward, toward the heartland, or further along the coast.
