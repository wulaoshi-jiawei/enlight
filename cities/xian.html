Xi’an

Xi’an is a city that looks back — toward the beginning of imperial China. Outside the walls, the Terracotta Army still stands in its ranks around the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor: thousands of soldiers, each with a face of its own, kept for a life after death. The Ming ramparts still circle the old city, wide enough to walk or cycle, and they give Xi’an a shape that has not been lost. In the Muslim quarter, the Great Mosque and its courtyards show how Islam took root here in a Chinese architectural language, among markets and food stalls that have been busy for centuries.

Arrive here and the west opens along the old Silk Road — Tianshui, Lanzhou, Zhangye, Jiayuguan, Dunhuang and Yumen Pass — and the heartland is already close. From Zhuxi, the road into Shaanxi comes first to Ankang, then on to Xi’an.

When to go & weather

Spring and autumn are the most comfortable times in Xi’an: clear days, and the best walking on the city wall. Summer is hot and dry; winter is cold, with a pale light over the ramparts.

Travel Guides

How to get there

By air

Xi’an Xianyang International Airport (XIY) lies north-west of the city, with a wide network of domestic flights and a growing number of international ones. The airport express and taxis run regularly into the centre.

By train

Xi’an North is the high-speed station, with frequent services to Beijing, Zhengzhou and the rest of the network, and slower lines onward toward the west. For travellers coming from Zhuxi, the road into Shaanxi comes first to Ankang, then on to the city.

Getting around

The metro now links the north station, the city wall and several of the principal sites. Taxis and Didi cover the rest. The Terracotta Army lies to the east of the city and is best reached with a driver or an organised transfer; the wall and the Muslim quarter are easily walked.

Best time to visit

April, May, September and October are the months to choose. Summers can be fiercely hot; winters are cold, though usually dry, and the pits and the wall are far less crowded. Avoid the early-October national holiday if you can: the warriors are then at their busiest.

Places Of Interest

The Terracotta Army

The buried guard of the first emperor remains one of the images by which the world first pictures China’s history: rank after rank of soldiers, horses and chariots, still being brought out of the earth. The scale is best understood in person; photographs, however good, make it smaller than it is.

The City Wall

The Ming ramparts still enclose the old city in a complete circuit, wide enough for a bicycle or a long walk at dusk. From the top, Xi’an reads as a planned city: gates, towers, and the urban grid that has been here for centuries.

The Muslim Quarter

North of the Drum Tower, the lanes of the Hui quarter are among the liveliest in the city: street food, market stalls, and, at their heart, the Great Mosque, founded in the Tang and rebuilt in a distinctly Chinese courtyard style.

The Big Wild Goose Pagoda

A Tang pagoda raised for the monk Xuanzang after his journey to India, and still one of the city’s great landmarks. The grounds around it are a pleasant place to walk in the late afternoon.

From Xi’an the Silk Road opens to the west, and the way back toward Wudang and Zhuxi is already close.
